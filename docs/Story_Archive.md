# Isles of Yum — Story Archive

A running log of every story written in this world, in order, with a short summary and craft notes (about the writing/structure, not the plot itself — plot and world details live in the World Guide). Useful for spotting patterns before starting a new story.

## 1. The Isles of Yum
**Cast:** Felix, Alex, Elfine, Marion, Mummy. **Length:** ~3,700 words (grew across several rounds of revision). **Islands Featured:** Fish Finger Reef (primary), Beans-on-Toast Bay (passing), Chocolate Mountain (passing), Candy Floss Clouds (primary), Strawberry Hill (mentioned, home of Mizzle's rain), Frozen Mango Isle (primary, cooldown stop).
**Summary:** First visit to the Isles. A storm caused by an unthanked, lonely cloud (Mizzel) threatens to ruin every island's food. The children learn saying thank you properly is a kind of magic; the story also includes a real boat-jeopardy rescue.

**What worked:**
- The "sad before bad" villain template, introduced here, became the backbone of the whole world's tone.
- Iterating in stages (outline → draft → add jeopardy → add resistance to the resolution → fix continuity) produced a much stronger story than the first pass — worth remembering that revision rounds add real value here, not just polish.
- Mummy shown as genuinely scared rather than infallible was a deliberate, successful correction partway through — made her a more interesting character.

**What to watch for next time:**
- The first draft had several continuity gaps that only surfaced on a full re-read (an ungrounded stowaway, a prop referenced before it was planted) — a dedicated "does every payoff have an earlier seed" pass is worth doing on any story with more than 2–3 setups.
- Early drafts had the villain's transformation resolve almost too fast — adding one round of "resistance" (Mizzel doubting the children's sincerity) before the resolution made the ending feel earned rather than instant.

## 2. The Map-Maker's Map (Part One)
**Cast:** Felix, Alex, Elfine, Marion + new recurring character Nib. **Length:** first attempt ~1,400 words (judged too thin); revised to ~2,430 words.
**Islands Featured:** Nib's sandwich island (primary, first appearance), Spaghetti Bolognese Island (primary, first appearance), Roast Dinner Isle (primary, first appearance).
**Summary:** Introduces Nib, a map-maker finishing her late mother's unfinished chart of the Isles. Two islands (Spaghetti Bolognese Island, Roast Dinner Isle) have faded from her map and need fixing; a near-drowning in a gravy river gives the story its jeopardy; the story ends on a genuine cliffhanger (an unrecognised symbol tied to Nib's mother).

**What worked (second attempt):**
- This is the clearest example in the archive of a first draft being diagnosed as structurally correct but texturally thin, then substantially rewritten rather than just polished — worth treating "this feels short" as a possible signal to add real depth (character backstory, sensory time in each location, a proper jeopardy set-piece) rather than just more incident.
- Giving Nib a real emotional motor (fear of failing to live up to her late mother) turned a functional new character into a genuinely affecting one, and did it through her own dialogue and a handwritten note rather than narrated backstory.
- This was your sons' most enthusiastically received story to date ("like watching TV") — notable that it had *three* distinct high-tension beats (a solvable problem, real physical jeopardy, an emotional cliffhanger) rather than the two-beat cap the process had been defaulting to. Directly led to revising the world guide's pacing rule.

**What to watch for next time:**
- The first attempt at this story under-delivered specifically by resolving both problems almost the instant the children arrived, with no time spent establishing what each place was like when things were working — a useful general check for future world-introduction stories: don't rush past the "ordinary day" texture on the way to the problem.

## 3. The Great Kitchen (Part Two)
**Cast:** Felix, Alex, Elfine, Marion + Nib. **Length:** ~2,830 words, written and reviewed in five sections rather than as one pass.
**Islands Featured:** Nib's sandwich island (passing, bookends), the Unmapped waters/mistake-islets (primary, first appearance — burnt toast, single-strawberry islet, steam-teapot), The Great Kitchen (primary, first appearance).
**Summary:** Pays off Part One's cliffhanger. The symbol marks the mythical Great Kitchen, source of every island in the Isles, which Nib's late mother spent her life trying to reach. A riddle-locked door, a journey through "mistake islands," and the discovery of the mother's wooden spoon resolve Nib's fear that she failed to live up to her mother's legacy — reframed instead as a deliberate, loving handover.

**What worked:**
- Writing in five discrete sections with a quick two-persona review after each (rather than one long draft followed by one review) caught tonal drift early — specifically, the abstractness of the door-riddle scene was flagged and fixed (anchored to a concrete callback from Part One) before it could compound into a bigger problem across the rest of the story.
- Deliberately varying which character got the big emotional insight (Felix, not Alex, delivers the tenderest line at the story's climax) was a good use of range — flagged explicitly as an intentional one-off rather than a new permanent pattern, which kept it feeling special rather than becoming a tic.
- A minor but real continuity error (a character's name used in narration before ever being introduced in dialogue) was caught on final read-through — a good reminder that even late-stage "finished" drafts benefit from one last continuity-only pass, separate from a craft/tone review.

**What to watch for next time:**
- This story's emotional stakes (grief, legacy) run considerably heavier than anything before it in the series — worth deliberately checking future high-concept emotional stories against the "does this still feel like Isles of Yum" tone bar, since ambition can pull a story toward a heavier register than the world's baseline warmth without anyone deciding that on purpose.

## 4. Marzipan's Well
**Cast:** Felix, Alex (no other family present). **Length:** ~1,650 words. **Islands Featured:** Satin Slopes / Rattlenut Rocks (primary, first appearance, single split island).
**Summary:** A nodding well between two feuding almond-butter villages (smooth-finish Satin Slopes vs. crunchy-finish Rattlenut Rocks) turns out to be worked from below by Marzipan, an enormous, unhurried sloth who has tended the shared spring for both villages, unthanked and unvisited, for longer than anyone can remember. Alex investigates alone and discovers Marzipan; a riddle delivered to both villages back on the surface gets them to realise who's really been "in every batch" — resolving both the villages' feud and Marzipan's quiet loneliness in the same beat.
**What worked:**
- No jeopardy beat at all, and no adult on-page — proved the two-hander (Felix + Alex only) format can carry a full, satisfying story on tension-type variety alone (mystery → social tension → mystery → emotional beat → problem-to-solve → resolution), with no two adjacent beats sharing a flavour.
- Established Felix's worry-almond/fidget-object device and his "interrupt-and-guess-wrong" joke (used exactly twice, always with an explicit dialogue tag like "Felix cut in" so it survives single-voice narration) — both now recorded as reusable Felix traits in the character-voice-guide reference.
- Alex investigating and solving almost entirely independently (climbing down alone, having the key emotional conversation, delivering the riddle) is a strong, true beat for his established "reserved observer who commits fully once he's worked out the shape of things" arc.

**What to watch for next time:**
- First draft had Felix go essentially silent for the entire ~75-line emotional core of the cavern scene (Marzipan's "nobody's ever asked" beat) despite being physically present — fixed on review by adding one small, wordless, physical Felix beat (leaning against Marzipan) rather than giving him a line that would have stepped on Alex's ownership of the insight. Worth checking any two-hander scene for this specific failure mode: a present-but-silent character during the story's emotional peak.
- The Marzipan/Felix interleaved dialogue beat ("Nobody's really ever—" / Felix's interruption / "...asked," Marzipan continuing) is the single hardest passage in the story to read aloud cold or split cleanly for multi-voice narration — flagged for extra attention at the `audio-narration-prep` stage specifically, not just general review.

## 9. Toffee Apple Orchard
**Cast:** Felix, Alex (no other family present). **Length:** ~1,550 words. **Islands Featured:** Toffee Apple Orchard (primary, first appearance), Amberdrift (primary, first appearance — new wandering-island device, born mid-story).
**Summary:** A jammed toffee vat floods part of the orchard; the boys evacuate every picker except Toffit, who knowingly chooses to stay with his trees. When the boys' own dam-building rescue attempt fails, Toffit builds and climbs into a toffee shell of his own making — a real, witnessed gamble, not a guaranteed plan — moments before the patch sinks. The patch surfaces again as Amberdrift, a new free-floating island, with Toffit alive inside the shell and about to become its travelling toffee-seller.
**What worked:**
- First use of the new World Guide craft rule 2a (the "unfixable loss, reframed rather than undone" exception): the *place* (the orchard patch) is the thing genuinely, permanently lost — the boys try everything and it still isn't enough — and that loss is resolved by reframing (Amberdrift) rather than fixing, a new affirmation shape for the series ("you can do everything right and it still won't go how you wanted, and that can still turn out okay").
- Deliberately the widest tension-flavour spread of any single-location standalone so far: wonder/arrival → mystery → diagnosis → organisational/evacuation → physical problem-solving → real loss/grief → mystery-relief → warmth/character reveal → quiet reflection, with no two adjacent beats repeating.
- Toffit's refusal to evacuate was added on review specifically so the loss would carry real human stakes, not just a place — an explicit choice not to write around ordinary human stubbornness ("there will always be people who don't want to leave their home, or listen to reason"). Planting his refusal during the evacuation (rather than revealing it only at the reveal) let his later survival land as relief rather than a fresh surprise.
- Produced a new recurring world device (Amberdrift, a free-floating island that's never in the same spot twice) and a new lightly-sketched recurring character (Toffit, now a travelling toffee-seller) in one story, both logged to the World Guide.

**What to watch for next time:**
- First draft had Toffit simply "go under with it" and resurface explained by an offstage detail (clung to a tree) the reader never saw — caught on a second review pass as a fake-out: it stated a person was lost, then explained the loss away with an unwitnessed trick, which undercut the honesty rule 2a depends on. Fixed by showing him build and climb into a toffee shell on-page, in front of the boys, before the patch goes under — real, witnessed risk with a visible mechanism, not a solemn "he's gone" beat that gets quietly reversed. Worth checking any future rule 2a story for this specific failure mode: if a beat plays as loss, don't let the undoing be something the reader wasn't shown.
- Craft rule 2a is an occasional exception, not a new default — the next few stories should return to the standard "effort resolves the jeopardy" pattern rather than reaching for the unfixed-loss shape again right away, or it will stop feeling special.
- The sink-to-surface sequence needed to stay tight (one continuous scene, no narrative distance) to keep the devastation beat from tipping into lingering dread for a young listener — worth the same discipline in any future use of rule 2a.

## 10. The Sweet Chain (Parts One–Three)
**Cast:** Felix, Alex (no other family present; Mummy appears only in bookend scenes). **Length:** Part 1 ~1,800 words, Part 2 ~1,550 words, Part 3 ~2,050 words (~5,400 words total, a 3-part serialized arc). **Islands Featured:** Liquorice Lane (passing, bookend shop), Ribbon Cove (primary, Part 1, first appearance), Simmer Slopes (primary, Part 2, first appearance), Canebrake Grove (primary, Part 3, first appearance). Also referenced but not visited: Biscuit Town, Strawberry Hill (both already established, shown as also depending on this supply chain).
**Summary:** Felix wants sweets; the shop's out of stock. A trail of clues leads the boys backward through the whole sugar-to-sweets production chain — a shaping/flavouring workshop (Ribbon Cove), a hillside of boiling cauldrons (Simmer Slopes), and finally the cane-growing source (Canebrake Grove) — with real sensory dwell-time at each stop, not just clue-hunting. Each stop reveals the shortage reaches one more already-established island (Biscuit Town's icing, then Strawberry Hill's jam), widening the stakes as they go. At the source, an old grower named Saffron has been secretly delaying harvest for weeks, afraid that his quick-learning apprentice Clove becoming equally skilled means he'll no longer be needed — a craft rule 1a "hoarding out of insecurity" mischief-maker, resolved through an Alex-delivered riddle rather than confrontation. A slapstick jeopardy beat (a shoe raced across a sticky spill, flattened by the mill's crushing rollers) gives Part 3 its one real scare, resolved instantly with zero injury and a lot of laughing.
**What worked:**
- First long-arc use of a literal supply-chain/production-line structure (source → processing → shaping → shop) as the spine of a mystery-trail story — a genuinely new structural pattern for this world, developed through several rounds of persona-review before outlining: concept review, then a "make it better" pressure-test pass (which added the branching-stakes device and settled on variable beat-counts per stop), then a framework-completeness pass (which caught that the arc had zero physical jeopardy across two of three parts and added the shoe/mill beat deliberately).
- "Branching stakes" is a new reusable device worth remembering: each part reveals exactly ONE additional named destination also depending on the same supply (Biscuit Town, then Strawberry Hill), using already-established islands rather than inventing new ones — cheap to write, rewards attentive repeat listeners, and gave the mischief-maker's resolution real emotional weight (Saffron realizing the true scope of who was waiting on him became part of his own arc, not a separate plot thread).
- The sticky-floor/flattened-shoe jeopardy went through an explicit persona-reviewed choice between two versions — a low-key "object pays the price" version versus a cartoon-logic "person gets rolled flat and reinflated" version — and the group unanimously rejected the second: it didn't actually satisfy the jeopardy rule's fear/teamwork/relief shape by its own design, and normalizing physical harm to a person as routine broke this world's tone precedent. Worth remembering as a real example of "funny idea, wrong world" for future jeopardy brainstorming.
- Following this arc's shoe-jeopardy with a self-imposed "hide it from Mum" kid-anxiety thread was deliberately resolved WITHOUT letting concealment work — Saffron tells them to just tell the truth, they do, and it's fine, echoing Story 1's honest-not-infallible-Mummy pattern. A useful template for any future story that wants a similar low-stakes kid-guilt beat.
- This arc prompted two new standing World Guide additions, discovered by checking this project's craft rules against general external story-structure guidance: (1) a new addendum to craft rule 2 distinguishing tension-*flavour* variety (existing) from stakes-*escalation* (new) — the single biggest/highest-stakes beat should land at or near the climax, not earlier; (2) an explicit statement that Felix and Alex are stable, non-arc protagonists by design (they apply established traits to solve problems; the world changes, they don't) — closing a gap that had never been decided one way or the other.
- A full-arc read-through after all three parts were drafted caught a real World Guide compliance gap that no single-part review had noticed: neither the opening nor the closing had an actual jigsaw-piece portal-activation scene (it had only been described as ambiently warm). Fixed by adding a proper glow/hand-hold bookend at the true start of Part 1 and the true end of Part 3 only — since the user clarified Isles-time runs independently of real-world time, a multi-part arc can be treated as one continuous Isles visit with bookends only at the very start and end, not per-part.
**What to watch for next time:**
- Each part needed a dedicated expansion pass to reach a reasonable word-count target — first drafts of Parts 1 and 2 came in noticeably short (~1,150 words) because dwell-time beats were sketched thinly on the first pass. Worth drafting dwell-time scenes with more initial texture next time, rather than relying on a planned later expansion pass.
- Two character names had to be renamed after an initial world-consistency-check: "Ribbon Reef" collided in naming-pattern with the existing Fish Finger Reef (no shared geography logic), and "Taffy" was too close in sound to the established Toffit. Both were caught before finalizing, but worth running the sound/pattern collision check earlier in the naming-workshop pass itself next time, not just at world-consistency-check.
- A cross-part continuity issue only became visible once all three parts existed together: Part 2's P.P.S. stated a conclusion ("not really anyone's fault") that Part 3 went on to reveal was wrong (it WAS a deliberate choice). Fixed by softening Part 2's line to read as a tentative guess rather than certainty. Worth deliberately re-reading every part's P.S./P.P.S. lines again once the whole arc is finished, since these short reflective lines are easy to leave stated as fact.
- This is the first Isles of Yum story where cast reasoning explicitly separates "stable protagonist" (Felix/Alex) from "the character who actually changes" (Saffron) — worth being deliberate about this distinction in any future story that features both a recurring emotional arc for a new/side character AND the two leads, so it's always clear whose arc is doing the changing.
