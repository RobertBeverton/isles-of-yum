# The Isles of Yum — World Guide & Story-Writing Process

A reference for writing new Felix & Alex stories (with supporting cousins/friends brought in as needed) set in the Isles of Yum food-world. Written so a new conversation can pick this up without needing prior chat history.

---

## THE WORLD

**Premise:** A magical world reached through a warm, glowing jigsaw piece (part of a puzzle in the family home). Each time a new piece is found and pushed into a gap, it pulls whoever is holding hands through a portal into the Isles of Yum — a sea dotted with islands, each themed around a food a 5-year-old British child would love. The piece is always warm, always glows gold, and always eventually pulls them home again, usually timed so almost no time has passed in the real world (a kettle just boiling, tea just being called).

**Tone:** Warm, gentle, funny, occasionally genuinely tense (real jeopardy, resolved by teamwork) — never grim, never features a true villain. Problems are caused by naturally-occurring mishaps (storms, breakages, mistakes, social misunderstandings) rather than antagonists with intent to harm. "Sad before bad" applies if anything ever seems villain-like — always look for the sympathetic, lonely, or misunderstood reason underneath.

**Recurring structural device (added in the "Right Order" story):** some islands' problems are interconnected and must be solved in a specific order — a nice occasional variant that turns a story into a live logic puzzle, good for interactive/choose-together read-alouds.

### Established Islands & Locations
- **Fish Finger Reef** — golden crispy cliffs, chip-shaped sand. Home of **Crispin**, a fish finger creature (named after initially being generic; recurring minor character, brave, dignified, once stowed away and helped save Felix from drowning).
- **Beans-on-Toast Bay** — water that's really slow-moving baked beans, toast-slice rocks.
- **Chocolate Mountain** — glossy brown slopes, a melted-chocolate river.
- **Candy Floss Clouds** — floating pink/blue candy floss islands, bounce gently, "SCHLOOP" when they degrade.
- **Strawberry Hill** — giant strawberries, a cold spring/stream behind a greenhouse, a beehive/jam operation.
- **Frozen Mango Isle** — cool, never-melting mango-ice hills; untouched by most crises, a good "reward/cooldown" stop.
- **Ice Cream Land** — gingerbread-style houses made of ice cream (wafer doors, chocolate-flake roofs). Home of the **Sorbet family** (Mr & Mrs Sorbet, two children) and neighbour **Mrs Cornetto**.
- **Chocolate Land** — three distinct villages: **Milk Chocolate Village** (cosy, warm), **Dark Chocolate territory** (tall, dignified, proud of being "not for everyone"), **White Chocolate towers** (pale, delicate, shy). Connected by a shared fountain landmark built jointly each year.
- **Biscuit Town** — round golden houses, biscuit-tiled roofs, ovens, a storeroom that stocks candy floss rope for towing.
- **Chocolate Ice-Cream Cove** — scoop-shaped hills of chocolate ice cream along a shoreline; vulnerable to melting without its shade-cloth mechanism.
- **Fluffy Candy (Candy Floss) Fields** — a source of very strong, stretchy candy floss, useful as rope/bridging material.
- **Spaghetti Bolognese Island** — noodle-garland hillsides, parmesan towers, powered by a **Meatball Fountain** at its heart.
- **Roast Dinner Isle** — Yorkshire pudding hills, roast potato boulders, a gravy river sourced from the **Gravy Boat**.
- **The Great Kitchen** — newly revealed mythical source of every island in the Isles, reached via a riddle-locked ancient oven door at the very edge of the map. Vast, golden, endlessly "unfinished" — new islands are still occasionally created there. Contains Cardamom's wooden spoon.
- **Satin Slopes** / **Rattlenut Rocks** — one island split cleanly in two: smooth, glossy honey-brown hills (Satin Slopes) facing craggy, boulder-strewn ground (Rattlenut Rocks), an almond-butter-producing island divided over smooth vs. crunchy finish. The two villages share one grove and one well between them. The sea around the island turns pale golden and syrupy near shore, warmed by fresh spring-water meeting the sea. At the island's centre, a nodding well-arm (worked from below by Marzipan) connects both villages to the spring underneath.
- **Toffee Apple Orchard** — rows of apple trees dipped whole (trunk, branches, leaves) in a shining amber toffee shell, with real apples underneath. Has one harvest day a year when every dipped apple ripens at once. Fed by a toffee vat and channel system that coats the trees; a jam in that system was the origin of **Amberdrift** (see below).
- **Amberdrift** — a small, free-floating island, born when a heavy, toffee-flooded patch of Toffee Apple Orchard broke away and sank, then resurfaced as its own detached piece of land. Never in the same spot twice — it drifts with the currents rather than being steered to a fixed place. A dozen toffee-dipped trees, a listing vat, and its resident **Toffit** (see Characters) live aboard it. A reusable recurring device: a place the children (or a future story) might have to go looking for rather than simply sail to.

- **Biscuit Town (expanded in "The Biggest Gingerbread Man in the Isles")** — also home of **Snap Day**, the town's yearly festival: the biggest Gingerbread in the Isles is baked and displayed on a barn-door-sized cooling rack in the square, and traditionally "snapped" at sundown so everyone gets a piece for luck. Since the Gingerbread woke up and ran, the tradition now is a **crumb-giving**: the Gingerbread stays whole and hands each townsperson a crumb with a hello. Crumble Hill (a steep hill of crumbs) is where children slide down on biscuit-tin lids. A hedge of shortbread; bunting piped in icing on shortbread flags.
- **Pizza Port** — a harbour and trading post on the far side of Beans-on-Toast Bay. Quay made of enormous round pizza bases laid edge to edge; boats tied up with stretched mozzarella; pepperoni coins as currency; stalls selling things on sticks. Gate sign: "Tolls Payable Before Panic". A **cheese-pull bridge** (a mozzarella cobweb on a great wooden crank) spans the red, bubbling harbour; it must never be hurried, and can be wound back as far as it will go and released as a slingshot (done once; a goat tried it before).

- **Crumpet Quay** — the Harbour Authority's flat, round, golden quay, covered in neat numbered holes; boats are tied through a hole, each with a pat of butter in it for luck. Berth Twelve is now Amberdrift's, with a sign ("AMBERDRIFT BERTH. (VACANT. PROBABLY.)") and a toffee-brown signal flag: run it up and wait. Berth One holds *Winkle*.
- **The Frozen Fruit Sound** — now visited on-page: a sea of striped fruit currents (strawberry, mango, peach, watermelon with seeds, raspberry) with low cold mist. Felix can identify currents by taste; the currents run in different directions and meet. Confuses compasses. No sensible sailor goes in.
- **Porridge Narrows** — a channel between two black rocks plugged by a hill of porridge, where Cockaigne seekers sit eating through it with spoons. Twelve chefs, a man in a bath and a goose in a deckchair. A channel has since been cut. The chefs went on to open a café there called *Probably*.
- **Breadstick Rock** — a tall pale rock like a breadstick, with a sunny ledge. Dry, so a good place for a fish finger.

### Travel & the Fleet
**The glowing puzzle piece is a required bookend, every story.** Confirmed favourite live-audience beat (one of the kids specifically lights up for it) — every story opens with the piece warming and glowing gold to pull Felix and Alex into the Isles, and closes with it glowing again to pull them home, timed so almost no time has passed in the real world. Don't skip or shortcut either end, even for a story that's otherwise a quick, single-location outing.

**Mid-story exception, for stories with a real quest/problem to solve:** if the children (or someone else) tries the piece before the story's problem is actually resolved, it can fizzle or sputter — warm, flicker, almost work — rather than fully activate. It doesn't glow enough to take them home until the quest/adventure is finished. This isn't a plot device to dwell on (a beat, not a subplot) but it's a natural, reusable way to explain why they can't just leave when things get hard, and it makes the final full glow at the end feel earned rather than arbitrary.

**The kids keep their pieces — a second, outside-world map, built alongside Nib's.** The very first piece (how Felix and Alex discovered the Isles at all) came from an existing, unrelated puzzle in the family home. Every piece since is kept once they're pulled home, and gets snapped into a new puzzle of the Isles of Yum that Felix and Alex are assembling themselves — a quick, mostly-wordless coda image at the end of a story (comparable weight to the P.S./P.P.S.), not a mechanic that needs explaining in dialogue. This is a slow background device, not something every story needs to reference, but it's available as a story hook: a piece gone missing or damaged can be why a previously-seen island is suddenly unreachable; a new piece can unlock only new territory.

This home puzzle is the **outside-world twin of Nib's map** — Nib's map is the in-world record (hand-drawn, in the Isles, re-inking itself as islands are tended or fade); the children's puzzle is the same picture of the Isles taking shape from the outside, one earned piece at a time. The two aren't the same object and don't have to move in lockstep, but they can occasionally echo each other for a specific story (something striking happening to one felt faintly in the other) — a rare, deliberate device, not a standing rule.

The Isles are still a real place with their own geography, reachable island-to-island once Felix and Alex are already there — the Fleet (below) is how they get around *within* a story or across an on-island stretch of an arc. The puzzle piece governs arrival from and return to the real world, not travel between islands.

**The Fleet — a progressive, earned set of vehicles, built up across stories rather than granted all at once (Thunderbirds/Octonauts/One Piece-style escalation).** The point of this device is that each new vehicle is a small story or arc in itself — earned through practice, failure, and improvement, not handed over fully-formed. Suggested rough progression (not fixed, adjust to taste as stories are written):
1. **A simple rowing boat** — the starting point, already established in earlier stories.
2. **A sail added** to the boat — a good "learning a new skill, wobbly at first" story in itself. **Done in "Wherever Amberdrift Got To":** Captain Crispin fitted a proper mast and sail to the boys' rowing boat (their only previous sail was a bedsheet) and taught them to tack ("a little left, a little right, and the wind thinks it has got its own way"). They can now sail freely in future stories without re-explaining; tacking into a headwind is earned.
3. **A motor** — faster, opens up further islands, maybe a small mishap-based lesson about going too fast or not steering properly yet.
4. **A bigger boat / proper vessel** — can carry more, maybe first vehicle that isn't kid-sized, could be gifted or built with help from an established character (Nib? Crispin?).
5. **Specialist "pod" tools** — kite-surfing, windsurfing, paragliding, a submarine, a small helicopter, etc. Each of these should get its own proper introduction story the first time it's used: real wobbling, falling, failing, gradually improving — never handed over as an instant, perfect new skill. Once earned, they can be used freely and briefly in future stories without re-explaining, the same way "they can row a boat" doesn't need re-litigating now.

**Craft rule this implies:** any brand-new vehicle or transport skill needs its own "learning it" beat somewhere — either as the spine of its introduction story, or as a proper opening sequence — before it can be used casually as connective tissue in later stories. Don't skip straight to competence.

- **Nib** — a small, frantic, endearing map-maker who lives on an island shaped like a half-eaten sandwich. Constantly drops/loses her own papers; visual tic of pencils tucked behind her ears. Deep character motor: she's finishing the mapping work of her late mother, **Cardamom**, the greatest map-maker the Isles ever had, and has spent her whole life quietly afraid she's failing to live up to that legacy.
- **Cardamom** (deceased, referenced not shown) — Nib's mother, spent years trying to reach the Great Kitchen. Left the final corner of the map unfinished on purpose, as something to hand down rather than a failure — the emotional reveal of Part 2. Left behind a wooden spoon, found inside the Great Kitchen itself.
- Nib's map is the series' emergent "index" of the whole world — islands can visibly fade from it if untended, and re-ink themselves when fixed. This is a strong ongoing device: **it gives a natural, low-effort reason for future adventures** ("the map shows a new blank patch...").
- Established tone arc for Nib: started fearful/scrambling → by end of Part 2, calmer, more confident, still exploring but "because she wants to, not because she's scared not to."

## CHARACTERS

**Cast priority (important default):** Felix and Alex are the core protagonists and the actual audience for these stories — every story exists for their enjoyment first. Elfine, Marion, and Mummy (and any future cousins or friends) are supporting cast, brought in deliberately when a specific story genuinely benefits from them (a bigger set-piece, a group dynamic the plot needs), not included by default or for the sake of "balance." A Felix-and-Alex-only story is not a reduced or lesser outing — it's the baseline the world is built around. When other characters are present, the existing "give every named character a job" rule still applies to them — but the first question for any new story should be "does this need more than Felix and Alex," not "how do we fit everyone in."

**Felix and Alex are stable protagonists by design, not arc characters.** Standard story-craft advice says a protagonist should be changed by their own story; this world deliberately doesn't work that way for its two leads. They apply their established, consistent traits (see `character-voice-guide`) to solve whatever the story's problem is — the world and its people change and grow (Nib, Marzipan, Toffit, Saffron), not Felix and Alex themselves. This is the correct shape for a long-running series with young repeat listeners, who want Felix to still *be* Felix next week, the same way a reader doesn't want Paddington to stop being Paddington. Don't force a personal-growth arc onto either boy to satisfy generic story-structure advice — it isn't a gap, it's a considered choice for this format.

The core family cast (Felix, Alex, Elfine, Marion, Mummy, Dad) is tracked in the separate **Isles of Yum - Core Cast** document, kept slim on purpose. World characters — Nib, Crispin, Mizzle, the Sorbet family, any future mischief-makers, etc. — are tracked below, alongside locations, and grow as new stories add them.

- **Marzipan** — an enormous, unhurried, kindly giant sloth who lives in a warm golden cavern beneath **Satin Slopes / Rattlenut Rocks**, tending the shared freshwater spring both villages draw from. Works the well's nodding arm personally, every day, for both villages equally — entirely unthanked and unvisited, since nobody had ever followed the sound of the well down to find out what made it move. Speaks slowly, in an unhurried, yawn-like cadence; doesn't mind being interrupted, just carries on once the interruption passes. Sad-before-bad pattern (craft rule 1a-adjacent, unthanked/lonely category, same shape as Mizzel) — resolved not by fixing a mistake but by the villages simply learning Marzipan exists and beginning a yearly thank-you tradition (a smooth almond and a crunchy almond left at the well's edge). A good candidate for a future irregular revisit.
- **Toffit** — a stocky, sticky-handed toffee-apple picker who spent thirty years working Toffee Apple Orchard, and knowingly refused to evacuate when his patch of the grove began to sink, choosing to take his chances rather than leave his trees. Survived the sinking by building himself a hardened toffee shell on the spot and climbing inside just before the patch went under — a real gamble on his own craft skill, not a guaranteed plan, and something the children watched him do rather than a fact revealed after the fact. Now lives aboard **Amberdrift**, the wandering island his choice created, working toward becoming its travelling toffee-seller (a sail, or perhaps an engine, in progress). Cheerful, immovable once decided, entirely unbothered by how things turned out compared to how he expected. Lightly sketched — a good candidate to develop further in a future Amberdrift story.

- **Humphrey Gingerly** — the enormous Festival Gingerbread of Biscuit Town's Snap Day, who woke up this year without anyone knowing why. Three gumdrop buttons (one sits slightly sideways since being fished out of Pizza Port harbour), white icing eyebrows, a slightly crooked piped smile. Says hello first, to everyone, and means it. Sheds crumbs when he laughs. Ran from the word "division", then chose to stay. Now the Guest of Honour on a gentler Snap Day. Light recurring potential: a friendly, gentle giant in Biscuit Town.
- **Mr Rusk, Snapping and Seconds** — a fox in a waistcoat, Biscuit Town's official Snapper for eleven years. Can clear a post office in one leap, speaks in stiff officialese when panicked, and had been left standing in a three-yard circle of empty cobbles at every festival because of his job. Sad-before-bad, "avoided because of the job title" category: he isn't alone, he's surrounded, and nobody sits next to him. Now "Mr Rusk, Sitting", at the Guest of Honour's right hand. Recurring potential as a very fast, very correct, increasingly warm helper.
- **Mrs Garibaldi, Head Baker and Opinions** — Biscuit Town's short, round, brisk head baker, with a rolling pin carried like a sword and an opinion on everything. Bakery sign: "Garibaldi's. Bakers. Established Before You."
- **Mr Capers, Tolls and Tutting** — Pizza Port's small, very broad toll-keeper in a tall paper hat, who tuts constantly (the tuts have meanings) and hasn't had a hello since "the Tuesday before last". Knees gone, so he handed the bridge crank to Felix and Alex. Keeps a drawer of lost buttons.
- **Commodore Bollard, Moorings and Regulations** — Harbour Authority, stiff, megaphone-and-clipboard, uniform with too many ribbons. Runs a fleet of twelve small red tugs named for bread rolls (the Bap, the Cob, the Barm, the Batch, the Stottie, the Bun, ...) whose crews argue about the names. Believes "Regulation One: every island must be somewhere", because at six he lost his own little red boat, *Winkle*, to the tide. Rule 1a "overprotectiveness tipping into control" type, not a lonely one; got his boat back from Toffit's island and now ties it up in Berth One with a loose bow so it can go if it wants. Recurring potential as a reformed stickler.
- **Captain Crispin, Crumb and Courage** — the established fish finger (see Fish Finger Reef), now also a dignified sailing instructor ("in an advisory capacity"). Spray makes him soft, so he can't cross open water ("I swam once. I was soft for a week"). Rescued from Breadstick Rock at dusk, perfectly crisp.
- **Toffit (update)** — his island collects whatever drifts to it (a wardrobe, a boot, *Winkle*); he says he "always turns up where somebody's got a face on". His sail is now properly rigged (the boys taught him), but he still doesn't steer unless someone runs the signal flag.
- **Deputy** — a seagull on Nib's windowsill wearing a tiny sash that says DEPUTY (promoted to SENIOR DEPUTY at the end of *Wherever Amberdrift Got To*). Never explained.

## CRAFT RULES THAT HAVE WORKED WELL

1. **No true villain, ever (so far).** Either: (a) a "sad, misunderstood creature" whose problem stems from being unappreciated/lonely (Mizzle), (b) a natural mishap/accident with no one at fault (broken bridge, jammed fountain, stuck boat), (c) a child's own mistake with real but fixable consequences (Felix eating the ice cream house), (d) a social/fairness problem between groups (Chocolate Land's three villages), or (e) a **cheeky mischief-maker** (see below, new category) — never a character whose intent is to cause real harm.

1a. **Cheeky mischief-makers — a new, distinct character category (available for long-arc stories, once a family is well established in this world).** The defining feeling this category should produce is: *"I do kind of get it — it's not right, but I do get it."* Different from the "sad-before-bad" Mizzle pattern (that character is hurting; this one is just a bit selfish or inconsiderate, not sad at all) and different from a natural mishap (there's a person making a choice here, not an accident). The understandable motive can take several different shapes — pick whichever fits the story, don't default to only one:
   - **Forcing a personal preference on everyone else** — loving a flavour, method, or way of doing things so much they impose it on a shared thing without asking (e.g. secretly seasoning communal food to their own taste).
   - **Cutting a corner out of impatience or pride** — wanting to finish first, win, or be seen as the best, and bending a rule slightly to make that happen, rather than any wish to hurt anyone.
   - **Hoarding or gatekeeping something out of a very understandable insecurity** — being so afraid of running out, or of not being special anymore, that they quietly keep more than their fair share, or won't share a skill/recipe/shortcut others need.
   - **Overprotectiveness that tips into control** — caring so much about doing right by something (a place, a tradition, a creature) that they stop anyone else from having a say in it at all.

   Whichever shape is used, the story should follow this arc:
   - **The tell:** something is subtly "a little off" rather than a full-blown crisis — a taste that's not quite right, a rule that's mysteriously changed, a shortage nobody can explain. This should read as curious/odd, not frightening, and suits a story where the kids get to *investigate* rather than stumble onto an obvious emergency.
   - **The catch:** the kids track down who's responsible and find a specific, human (or creature-shaped), understandable reason behind it — not malice, just a preference, an insecurity, or an impulse taken slightly too far.
   - **The conversation, not a punishment:** resolve it through dialogue, not defeat. A child character should say the actual lesson plainly and kindly — something in the shape of *"It's fine that you [want/love/feel] that. It's not fine to [force it on/take it from/decide it for] everyone else without asking."* The character isn't shamed for the underlying want, only asked to stop overriding other people because of it.
   - **A dignified resolution, not just a scolding:** wherever possible, give the character a legitimate outlet for the want that caused the problem — a dedicated space, a proper turn, an acknowledged role — so the story ends with their underlying feeling respected, just no longer allowed to run over everyone else.
   - **Recurring potential:** once caught-and-understood, a mischief-maker can become an irregular semi-regular the kids visit later for a specific favour or specialist knowledge tied to whatever they were originally over-enthusiastic about — today's minor mischief becoming tomorrow's useful contact, once the boundary has been respected.
   - **Tone guardrail:** keep it warm, funny, and a little silly — never moralistic or punitive. The character should stay likeable throughout; they're corrected on one specific behaviour, never made to feel bad about who they fundamentally are.


2. **Vary the type of tension across a story; don't cap the number of exciting moments.** Before adding or trimming a beat, check it against this list:
   - What *type* is this beat — problem-to-solve, physical jeopardy, social/emotional tension, or a mystery/cliffhanger?
   - Does the beat immediately before it share the same type? If yes, either change this beat's type or cut it to a shorter texture moment instead of a full set-piece.
   - Is there a reason beyond "more excitement" for this beat to exist (does it move character, plot, or the world forward)?

   A story can have three, four, or more high points as long as each is a different flavour of tension — repeating the *same* flavour back-to-back is the actual failure mode, not the total count. (A story with three consecutive push-and-solve puzzles will drag; a puzzle, then a real scare, then an emotional gut-punch will not.)

   **Flavour variety and intensity are two different checks — satisfy both.** Varying tension-type (above) stops beats from feeling repetitive; it doesn't by itself guarantee the story builds. Separately check that the single highest-stakes or most emotionally weighty beat lands at or near the climax, not earlier — a story where the scariest or most affecting moment happens in the middle, then cools off before the ending, will feel like it peaked too soon even if every beat was a different flavour. This doesn't mean every beat before the climax must be smaller than the one after it, only that the story's *biggest* beat shouldn't be spent early.

   Whenever a beat is genuine physical jeopardy (a near-drowning, a slip toward danger), apply these details regardless of how many such scenes the story has: cause it by environment or chance, not malice; resolve it within about a page; let the children's own teamwork solve it, with any adult present but not the one who fixes it; end in relief or laughter, not lingering dread; and give the fright one quiet "catching your breath" beat before resuming plot momentum.

   2a. **Exception — the unfixable loss, reframed rather than undone (occasional, not default).** Every jeopardy beat above assumes the children's effort saves the thing at risk. It's fine, occasionally, to let the effort genuinely fail — the thing is lost — provided the loss is immediately reframed into something new and good, not left as a flat defeat. This is a different resolution shape from "resolve within about a page," and needs its own calibration: let the children try everything first (so the loss isn't for lack of trying), let them and any onlookers feel real, brief devastation, then cut to the reframe fast — within a page of the loss itself, not lingering. The reframe must be concrete and visibly positive (the lost thing becomes something new, not just "it's OK really"), so a young listener is never left holding unresolved sadness for more than a beat or two. The affirmation this earns — *you can do everything right and it still won't go the way you wanted, and that can still turn out okay* — is worth the occasional use precisely because it's not available anywhere else in the standard jeopardy pattern. Don't overuse it: it works because the normal pattern (effort fixes it) is the baseline the exception is allowed to break. If a person (not just a place or object) is the thing at risk, don't state or imply they're lost and then explain their survival with something the reader never saw — show whatever kept them safe on the page, before the loss lands, so the devastation beat is honest rather than a fake-out that gets quietly reversed (caught and fixed in Toffee Apple Orchard's first draft).
3. **Give every named character a job.** With 4 kids, make sure each one plans, acts, notices, or comforts at some point — check at review stage that no one has gone silent for a long stretch. This isn't a strict rotation quota; it's a check that nobody's forgotten.
4. **Let the story's own stakes decide how many locations or set-pieces it needs; don't pre-cap it.** Before adding a new location or set-piece, check it against this list:
   - Does this introduce a genuinely new *flavour* of challenge (different from any other stop already in this story)? If yes, it earns a full stop. If no, shrink it to a texture beat instead (a joke, a smell, a quick line of dialogue) rather than a full problem-and-solution scene.
   - Does the story still have light-and-shade — at least one calmer, lower-intensity stretch — even if every beat is exciting? A story that is wall-to-wall high-intensity beats with no breathing room can overstay its welcome even without a numeric cap; pacing still needs contrast, just not a beat-counter.

   Small, single-location stories (Ice Cream Land: one mistake, one fix) and sprawling multi-leg expeditions (The Great Kitchen: journey, threshold puzzle, revelation) are both correct outcomes of applying this checklist — the right scope depends on the ambition of the story, not a fixed number.
5. **Plant, then pay off.** Anything used for a punchline or twist late in the story (a stowaway, a prop, a piece of information) needs an earlier, light seed. Don't introduce a payoff with no setup (this was caught and fixed twice: Willow the cat's stowaway logic, and the "propped jigsaw piece" reference in Ice Cream Land).
6. **Use concrete details, not abstract ones, especially for younger listeners.** When a story reaches for a bigger, more thematic idea (e.g. the door riddle in Part 2), anchor it with a plain, concrete callback to something the children have physically already done in this world, so the abstraction has a foothold.
7. **Write exactly two short postscripts.** P.S. = a laugh/comic coda. P.P.S. = a quiet emotional or reflective beat, often a line from Mummy or between the kids, landing the story's takeaway without preaching it as a moral.
8. **Let characters live the affirmation through plot; never state it as a narrator's moral.** The lesson (persistence, honesty about fear, working together, fixing your own mistakes, the right order matters, legacy/starting something you can't finish, doing everything right and it still not working out) should be something a character does or says in scene, not a closing summary sentence.

**Long-form stories** add rules L1–L11 (see *Long-Form Stories*); they refine, never override, rules 1–8. The one to watch: a long-form "taker" may be spectacular (a shapeshifter, a chase) but rule 1 still holds: no one acts with intent to cause real harm.

**On precedence:** rules 2–4 (tension variety, character jobs, scope) are soft constraints to satisfy together during outline review, not a strict hierarchy — if a choice serves one better at a small cost to another, that's an acceptable trade. Rules 1, 5, 6, 7, and 8 are closer to hard requirements and shouldn't be traded away. Rule 2a is an occasional, deliberate exception to rule 2's default jeopardy shape, not a competing rule — invoke it on purpose for a specific story, don't drift into it by default.

## LONG-FORM STORIES (THE ~30-MINUTE STORY)

The standard stories above run 15–20 minutes narrated. The boys now enjoy ~30-minute stories, so long-form is a first-class format here: **~4,200–5,000 words, ~28–33 minutes narrated** (single-voice TTS runs roughly 145 words/minute). Longer is not "a standard story stretched" — it needs more *structure*, not more of the same. This section is the blueprint; the craft rules below still apply on top of it.

**Reference models** (in `reference/`, both ~4,500 words, 16–17 chapters): *The Frost Who Asked "Are You Warm?"* and *The Singer Who Sailed the Pond*. They come from the separate Stories repo — school setting, different cast and canon. **Borrow the craft, never the characters, setting, or lore** (no Baba Yaga, Willow, Father Frost etc. in the Isles).

### What those two stories do (and how to translate it)

| Technique | In the references | In the Isles |
|---|---|---|
| **Micro-chapters** | 16–17 short titled chapters, one scene each, ~200–350 words | Same. Every chapter is a natural pause point for the listener and (see below) a clean narration chunk |
| **A folklore anchor** | A "fact" chapter teaches a real myth (Morozko; the Kalevala's Sampo) in kid-sized language, then the story plays with it and *twists* it | Anchor each story in a real food/feast/hospitality tale (see ideas below), explained in plain, concrete terms, then inverted or completed |
| **A refrain that changes meaning** | "Are you warm?" opens the story, then returns asked *by the children*, answered honestly, and finally asked back by the child who was rude | One short, sayable phrase from chapter 1 that returns 3+ times; its final use flips who asks or answers |
| **Braided threads** | Quest/rescue (A) braided with a smaller human thread — a rude child, a girl who melts in spring (B) | One external problem plus one small human thread that gets its own quiet chapter *after* the climax |
| **Plant, use, lose** | The mill is shown working in chapter 3, stolen in chapter 5 | The central object is introduced *and used on the page* within the first ~20%, before it's taken or broken |
| **Obstacle ladder** | Pike (ask nicely + sing), guards (lullaby), nine locks (skill), eagle (tug of war) | 3–4 obstacles, each a different flavour and a different virtue (see L6) |
| **Handover** | The old singer's hands freeze; the twins take the instrument — first note wobbly, third note clear | The wise helper hits a limit and Felix and Alex take over something new, with a believable learning beat |
| **Sympathetic taker with real menace** | Inspector Lowhee steals the Sampo and turns into an enormous eagle, then admits "nobody ever invites the North" | Real spectacle is allowed; real harm is not. The reason is sad or understandable and gets spoken plainly |
| **The break that becomes the meaning** | The Sampo shatters and never returns; the "real" magic was everyone turning the handle together | Pairs naturally with rule 2a: the thing is lost, and the reframe is the story's heart |
| **Grown-up wit** | Badges with job titles ("Choir and Sauna"), deadpan officials, a bear in the car park | Light, deadpan comedy for the reading parent, never at the expense of the child's momentum |

### The shape: ~16 chapters

Word budgets are guides, not quotas. Each chapter has **one scene, one beat, one job**, and ends on a turn — a line, a question, an image — that pulls the listener into the next.

1. **Open (chapters 1–3, ~20%).** The piece warms and glows (required bookend) → arrival → a strange new figure/place/question that sets the *refrain*. Within these chapters: the **fact chapter** (the folklore anchor) and the **plant** (the central object shown working).
2. **Inciting turn (chapters 4–5, ~12%).** The object is taken, broken, or lost, or someone is in trouble. Stakes become personal for a named resident.
3. **Quest (chapters 6–10, ~28%).** Travel and the obstacle ladder. Include at least one calm, funny, low-intensity chapter between set-pieces.
4. **Climax (chapters 11–13, ~20%).** The handover, then the biggest beat in the story (rule 2's "don't spend the biggest beat early" applies with extra force over 30 minutes).
5. **Resolution (chapters 14–16, ~20%).** The sympathetic reason spoken aloud → the kids' concrete, doable repair task → the reveal of what the magic *really* was → the B-thread's quiet chapter.
6. **Coda.** P.S. (comic) and P.P.S. (quiet), then the puzzle piece glowing them home and the piece snapping into the home puzzle. A "hook" for next time is optional: a blank patch appearing on Nib's map, or a new piece glowing.

### Long-form rules

- **L1. Chapters are the unit of pacing.** 150–400 words each, one scene each. If a chapter has two scenes, split it; if it has no turn at the end, rework it. Keep each under ~350 words where practical so it fits one ElevenLabs batch (2,000-character limit) without splitting mid-scene.
- **L2. One refrain, used on purpose.** A short phrase or question, kid-sayable, that is *asked* first by someone with authority or mystery, *returns* with the children's own spin, and *lands* last with a changed meaning. It is the long-form version of rule 8: the lesson lives in the repeated line, never in a narrator summary.
- **L3. Folklore anchor, explained in one chapter, then played with.** Give the tale in plain concrete terms (rule 6), in the voice of someone natural to deliver it (a resident elder, Nib's map notes, a story Mummy or Nana told before the piece glowed). Then do something with it: invert it, complete it, or show the real story underneath. Never retell it for its own sake. Ideas that suit this world: *Stone Soup*, *The Gingerbread Man*, the land of Cockaigne (a land where food is free and everywhere), the cornucopia/horn of plenty, *The Magic Porridge Pot*, the Sampo-style endless mill, the feast that needs every guest to bring something.
- **L4. Two threads, braided.** The A-thread is the external problem; the B-thread is a small human situation (a boastful newcomer, a resident who can't be with the others, someone afraid of an ending). B starts early, takes a back seat during the quest, and gets its quiet payoff *after* the climax.
- **L5. Plant by chapter 3, pay off in the last third.** Long stories give more room to forget a plant. At outline stage, list every plant with its payoff chapter.
- **L6. The obstacle ladder.** 3–4 obstacles, each a different *flavour* of tension (rule 2) **and** a different *virtue or skill*: at least one solved by courtesy or kindness rather than force, one by a specific practised skill, one by pure teamwork. No two in a row solved the same way. Felix and Alex stay themselves (no growth arc, per the Characters section); the *obstacles* vary, not the boys.
- **L7. The handover needs a learning beat.** If the wise helper hands Felix and Alex a task they've never done, it can't succeed at once — first note wobbly, third note clear, and the helper says why they can (e.g. "a song is just a feeling put in order"). Same logic as the Fleet craft rule.
- **L8. A taker, never a harmer.** The antagonist-shaped character may be big, loud, a shapeshifter or a chaser, because spectacle is good fun. But their act is *taking* (hoarding, keeping, claiming), never hurting; their reason is one a child can say back ("nobody invited them"); and the resolution is a conversation plus a small, concrete job. Craft rule 1a's tone guardrail applies in full: they stay likeable, corrected on one behaviour, never shamed. Jeopardy beats still follow rule 2's calibration (environment or chance, children solve it, relief or laughter, one breath-catching beat).
- **L9. Light and shade, at length.** Over 30 minutes, check for: at least two pure-comedy chapters, at least one quiet/tender chapter before the climax, and no run of three chapters at the same intensity. This is rule 4's light-and-shade check with a higher bar.
- **L10. Give the draft texture on the first pass.** The Sweet Chain's first drafts came in ~1,150 words against a ~1,800 target because dwell-time beats were sketched thinly. For long-form, write the "nothing is happening yet" chapters (arrival, a funny local, a smell, a meal) with real texture the first time; don't rely on a later expansion pass.
- **L11. Felix and Alex remain the baseline.** The references have a full class of children; the Isles version doesn't need them. Extra length comes from **more places, more residents, more obstacles**, not more family. Bring in Elfine, Marion or Mummy only if a specific chapter needs them (rule 3 still applies to anyone present).

### Narrative voice: the silly register

The boys love the *comic style* of the reference stories, separately from their school setting. It layers on top of the Isles' existing warm, sensory narration (the toffee-scented openings, Felix licking things); it doesn't replace it, and it works in any length of story, not only long-form. Its ingredients:

- **Deadpan understatement.** The narrator reports absurd things flatly and never winks. ("He knelt down, which took a while because of his knees." / "'Two minutes,' said Posy. 'Which is a lot.'")
- **An absurd detail, left unexplained.** Something ridiculous just *is* there, and a character's single question gets a "Sh." in reply. (*"Why's there a bear?" "Ssh."*) In the Isles this might be a dignified fish finger queueing patiently for nothing, or a gravy-boat crew solemnly saluting a sandwich.
- **Labels and job titles as gags.** Characters get a badge-style deadpan tagline when first met ("Mister Vyno, Choir and Sauna"). Isles equivalents: shop signs, name-cards, hand-lettered notices ("Mrs Cornetto, Cones and Complaints").
- **Officious grown-ups with a funny, quotable line.** ("Tea is for people who have time to be warm.") Good fodder for the reading parent and a clean place to hide a plant.
- **Rule of three with a twist on the third**, and **literal-minded child logic** ("I'm warm and I've got a snowman." / "Is it naughty and nice?").
- **Short sentences, short paragraphs, one-line punchlines.** This also suits single-voice narration, where the joke must live in the text rather than in a performance (see `persona-review`'s narration-format persona). Keep scene-setting sentences long and lush at the *start* of a chapter, then tighten once the jokes begin.
- **Funny right up to the tender beat, then plain.** The reference stories pivot from comedy to feeling with one flat sentence and no joke afterwards ("'Nobody has asked me that,' said Father Frost, 'in a hundred years.'"). Never undercut a real emotional beat with a gag.
- **Nobody knows they're funny.** Residents are completely serious. The comedy comes from the narrator's calm and the kids' reactions.

**Keep Isles canon where it conflicts:**
- Third-person narrator, not the references' first-person "we". Felix and Alex stay distinct per `character-voice-guide` (Felix impulsive and physical, Alex observant, riddling); no automatic "said both" duet lines unless a story earns one on purpose.
- No guest cast imported from the references (no class, teachers, mythical cameos from another canon).
- The silliness never overrides rule 1's tone guardrail: likeable characters, no one shamed, jeopardy still calibrated.

### Long-form checklist (run at outline stage and again at final read)

- [ ] ~16 chapters, each with one beat and a turn at the end; a chapter-by-chapter list with word budget and tension-type tag exists
- [ ] Refrain appears in chapter 1, returns 3+ times, and flips at the end
- [ ] Folklore anchor delivered in one short concrete chapter, then twisted or completed
- [ ] Central object shown working before it goes wrong (plant by ~20%)
- [ ] Obstacle ladder: no two adjacent obstacles share a flavour or a solution
- [ ] Handover has a learning beat
- [ ] Taker is sympathetic, menace stays spectacle (no real harm), reason spoken plainly, repair job concrete
- [ ] Biggest beat is in the final third, not the middle
- [ ] B-thread gets a quiet chapter after the climax
- [ ] Comic register present (deadpan, an unexplained absurd detail, a title/label gag, short punchline lines) without undercutting the tender beats
- [ ] Puzzle-piece glow opens *and* closes; P.S. and P.P.S. both present
- [ ] Chapters break with `• • •` (the narration pipeline splits on this), and no heading text will be read aloud unintentionally

**Chapter titles are optional.** The reference stories title each chapter ("Part Five. Taken for Inspection"). A `## Part N. Title` heading is read aloud as a spoken line and gives the app its part list; scenes still break on `• • •`. Write chapters with `• • •` breaks and no titles (matches existing Isles stories) or with `## Part N. Title` headings, as wanted. Also avoid the word "Part" for internal chapters: it's already used for serialized series (*The Sweet Chain, Part Two*).

## THE WRITING PROCESS THAT'S WORKED

For a new story:

1. **Brainstorm/outline first.** Pick location(s), the core problem (non-villain), and one clear affirmation/theme. Sketch character jobs before writing prose.
2. **Quick two-persona check on the outline** before drafting:
   - 🧒 **Five-year-old listener** — is it fun, is it clear, is anything confusing or scary in the wrong way?
   - ✏️ **Editor** — structure, tension-type variety (no repeated beat-type back to back), villain rules, character balance, jeopardy calibration.
3. **Write the full draft.**
4. **Run at least one full review pass, sometimes two in sequence** (second pass checks the first pass's suggested fixes against the *whole* story before applying them — this catches suggestions that look good in isolation but would over-stuff or contradict something elsewhere).
5. **Apply only the fixes that survive the second pass.** Not everything flagged needs fixing — distinguish "real gap" from "minor and net-neutral."
6. **For long/ambitious stories, draft in sections and review each section as it's written**, then do one final full read-through pass at the end to check overall flow, plants/payoffs, and character balance across the whole thing (this is how Part 2 / The Great Kitchen was built).
7. **Word count is a target, not a hard limit** — several of the best stories (Ice Cream Land, Chocolate Land) hit their mark around 1,400-1,450 words; the ambitious two-part Nib arc ran to ~2,400 and ~2,800 words because the story genuinely needed the space (proper world-building, character depth, one real jeopardy set-piece, an earned emotional climax). Err toward giving a story the length it needs rather than trimming for a round number. **Long-form (~4,200–5,000 words, ~30 minutes) is now a deliberate target for the next stories** — see *Long-Form Stories* above for its structure.
8. **For long-form, outline by chapter before any prose.** A numbered list of ~16 chapters, each with a one-line beat, a tension-type tag (problem / physical / social-emotional / mystery / comedy / calm), a rough word budget, and the plant/payoff map. Run the outline through `persona-review` and `world-consistency-check` as usual; the chapter list makes the "no repeated beat-type back to back" check mechanical. Then draft in thirds (open + inciting; quest; climax + resolution + coda), reviewing each third, and finish with one full read-through.

## FORMAT VARIANTS TRIED (all successful, worth reusing)

- **Standard read-aloud story** — straight narrative, ~800–3000 words depending on ambition (15–20 minutes narrated at the upper end).
- **Long-form story** — ~4,200–5,000 words (~30 minutes narrated), ~16 short chapters, a folklore anchor, a recurring refrain, and braided A/B threads. Full blueprint in *Long-Form Stories*. Modelled on the two stories in `reference/`. First used in the Isles with *The Biggest Gingerbread Man in the Isles*.
- **Interactive "choose together" version** — same story skeleton, but paused at decision points where the kids pick what happens next live. Wrong choices trigger an in-world "reset" (the jigsaw piece flickers and rewinds time to the same choice point) rather than a dead end or real failure — keeps it low-stakes and encourages trying again. Best used for stories built around a genuine order-dependent puzzle.
- **Multi-part serialized arc** — a bigger mystery (Nib's map, the unexplained symbol) seeded in Part 1 and paid off properly in Part 2, with its own character arc (Nib) running underneath the episodic adventures.
- **Vehicle-learning story** (new format, not yet used) — a story built around Felix and/or Alex learning a new mode of travel from scratch (wobbling, falling in, slowly improving), ending with them earning proper use of it for future stories. First candidate: learning to sail or kite-surf.

  - *Forcing a preference:* a resident obsessed with pickled onion flavour secretly sneaks it into some of the biscuits at Biscuit Town, genuinely believing everything's better with a bit of pickled onion. Kids notice a few batches taste "a little off," investigate, and catch them in the act. Resolution: a labelled pickled-onion corner of the bakery, so their taste is celebrated without being forced on anyone else. Good candidate to recur later as a flavour specialist.
  - *Impatience/pride:* a young resident desperate to win the Chocolate Land fountain-building competition (or similar) two years running quietly starts the build a day early, breaking the "everyone starts together" tradition, not out of malice but pure competitiveness. Kids notice the head start, investigate why it felt unfair even though nothing was technically "stolen," and the resolution turns on the difference between wanting to win and needing to win fairly.
  - *Hoarding out of insecurity:* an ageing resident who used to be the only one who knew a particular recipe or shortcut refuses to teach it to anyone, secretly afraid that once everyone can do it, nobody will need them anymore. Resolution involves the kids gently showing them that being generous with what you know doesn't make you less special.
  - *Overprotectiveness tipping into control:* a resident so devoted to protecting a fragile island or tradition that they've started making every decision about it alone and shutting everyone else out "for its own good." Resolution involves helping them see that caring about something means sharing responsibility for it, not owning it alone.
