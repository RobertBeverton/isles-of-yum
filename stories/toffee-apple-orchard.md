The Toffee Apple Orchard smelled like the inside of a warm oven that had
somehow also grown a forest. Rows and rows of apple trees stood in neat
lines, each one dipped — trunk, branches, leaves and all — in a shining
amber toffee shell that caught the sun like glass. Real apples hung
underneath, waiting to be picked, dipped fresh, and sold before the shell
cooled hard.

"It's like the trees got dressed up," said Felix, prodding a low branch
with one finger and then licking it, immediately, without checking if
anyone had said that was allowed.

"That's the whole point of them," said Alex, hanging back at the edge of
the grove the way he always did in a new place, letting his eyes travel
slowly along the rows before he stepped properly inside. "Nobody has to
tell you the rule. You can just see it."

They'd come for the Orchard's one harvest day — the single morning each
year when every dipped apple on every tree was ready at once, and the
whole grove had to be picked, boxed, and sent off to the harbour stalls
before the afternoon sun softened the shells. Felix had a small woven
basket looped over one arm. Alex had brought nothing but was already
counting trees under his breath.

They were three rows in, Felix eating slightly more than he was
collecting, when they heard it — a low, wet groan, like a very large
door easing open somewhere underground.

"That wasn't a tree," said Alex, going still.

It came again, longer this time, and under their feet the ground gave the
smallest possible lurch, like a boat catching a wave side-on.

They found the cause two rows further down. A great copper vat, taller
than either of them, had been feeding fresh toffee down a channel to coat
that morning's last unpicked row — except the channel had jammed solid,
and instead of running where it should, the toffee had backed up and
spilled, thick and slow, pooling deep across the lowest stretch of the
orchard floor. It wasn't just sitting there. It was still pouring. And the
whole patch beneath it — trees, ground, everything — had begun, very
slowly, to tilt.

• • •

Felix's basket hit the floor. "Is it sinking?"

"I think it's the weight," Alex said, crouching to press a flat hand
against the ground, feeling for the tremor properly instead of just
looking at it. "All that toffee, pooling in one place. It's heavier than
this bit of the island was ever meant to hold."

A creak rolled up through the grove, long and groaning, like the whole
patch clearing its throat.

Neither of them needed telling what to do next. "Everyone out," Felix
bellowed, already running for the nearest row of pickers, waving both
arms over his head. "Out, out, everybody out, just in case!"

They went tree to tree, row to row, Felix loud and fast at the far end
of the grove, Alex calm and clear at the near end, steering pickers and
their loaded baskets away from the tilting ground and up onto the solid
orchard path above it. An old picker with toffee to his elbows wouldn't
leave his half-full basket behind until Alex, without raising his voice
at all, said, "The apples will still be apples tomorrow. Come on." That
worked better than shouting would have.

Only one picker wouldn't budge at all — a stocky, sticky-handed man
already down among the lowest, worst-tilting trees, calmly checking each
trunk as though the ground beneath it weren't creaking with every
breath.

"You have to get off," Felix told him, hauling at his sleeve. "It's
going to sink!"

"Might," the man agreed, not moving an inch. "Thirty years I've dipped
apples on this patch. I'll take my chances with it."

"That's not — that's not how chances work," Alex tried, but the man was
already waving them both off, cheerful and entirely immovable, and there
was no more time to argue him out of it. They counted every other
picker safely up onto the path — Alex mouthing the number twice to be
sure — and left him where he stood, because some choices, once a person
has truly made them, aren't really anyone else's to unmake.

"We can still stop it," Felix said. "Can't we?"

Alex was already moving, and this time there was no hanging back at all.
He went straight for the jammed channel, hands finding the crusted lever
at its side, testing it, throwing his whole weight against it while Felix
grabbed the vat's tipping-wheel and hauled the other way. It didn't
budge. Felix kicked a wedge of hardened toffee free from underneath the
channel — that helped, a little, the flow slowing — but the lever stayed
locked fast, and behind them the ground gave another lurch, worse than
the last.

"Bank it," Alex said, thinking out loud now, fast. "If we can't stop the
pour, we build a wall — bank the toffee away from the low ground, spread
the weight out—"

They tried. They really tried — hauling loose stones and fallen branches
into a rough dam, packing wet sand from the shoreline path against it,
Felix throwing himself into the work with his whole body the way he threw
himself into everything, Alex directing where each stone should go with
the same care he gave to stacking his Minecraft walls at home, one
block, one decision, at a time.

Further up the slope, the sticky-handed man wasn't checking trees any
more. He'd dragged a fallen crate under the biggest one and was packing
it round, thick and fast, with double-handfuls of half-set toffee
straight from the pooling flood — patching the gaps, smoothing the
sides, working with the easy confidence of someone who'd shaped toffee
with his hands for thirty years and trusted it completely.

"What is that?" Felix shouted up at him.

"A shell!" the man shouted back, not stopping. "Toffee sets hard as
anything once it's cooled. Trust me — I make my living out of this
stuff." He climbed inside it, knees to his chest, and pulled the lid of
woven branches down over the top of himself just as the ground beneath
the dam finally gave way completely.

The patch broke free with a sound neither of the boys ever quite found
the right word for afterwards — not a crash, not a snap, but something
low and final, like the island itself letting out a breath it had been
holding. The shell went under with everything else — the trees, the
toffee, the dam, the whole tilted stretch of orchard sliding from the
rest of the island into the water in a wash of white foam and amber
spray.

Then it was just sea, rocking gently where solid ground had been a
moment before, one small toffee-shelled shape bobbing somewhere
underneath it, out of sight.

Nobody said anything. The picker who hadn't wanted to leave his basket
had his hand pressed flat over his mouth. Felix stood with his fists
still closed around nothing, the tipping-wheel long gone under the
water with everything else. Alex didn't move at all, staring at the flat
place where the trees — and the shell, and the man inside it — had
been, and for once he didn't have a riddle for it, or an answer, or
anything to say.

"We tried everything," Felix said eventually, in a small voice that
didn't sound like his at all.

"I know," said Alex, not taking his eyes off the water. "He built that
shell so fast. He must have already known he might need it."

They stood there a long moment, the whole grove silent behind them,
watching the empty water. It was, Felix thought, the worst kind of quiet
— the kind where trying your absolute hardest still hadn't been the
end of the story he'd wanted.

• • •

And then the water broke.

Not a wave — a rising. Amber and green and still faintly steaming, the
whole lost patch came surging back up out of the sea in a rush of foam,
tipped at a jaunty new angle, trees and toffee and all, and settled on
the surface of the water like it had simply decided to float there
instead.

"IT'S BACK!" Felix was already sprinting for the shoreline, and this
time nobody stopped him.

It wasn't reattached to the rest of the Orchard — it simply floated free,
entirely its own island now, bobbing gently a little way offshore and
trailing a faint ribbon of steam where warm toffee met cool sea air.
Wedged between two trees, dented but whole, sat the toffee shell —
and even as they watched, its woven lid shoved upward and off, and the
same sticky-handed man who had refused to come with them climbed out,
soaked through, grinning enormously, and raised one hand in a cheerful
wave.

"TOFFIT!" someone on the shore bellowed, half laughing, half furious.
"You absolute menace, we thought you'd gone under with it!"

"I DID go under with it," the man called back, delighted, thumping the
side of the dented shell like an old friend. "Told you toffee sets hard
as anything! Knew that shell would hold the second the ground started
going — should've built it the moment you two started shouting at me,
if I'm honest."

"That shell might not have held," Alex called back, half a laugh and
half something more serious underneath. "You didn't know it would.
Not really."

"Didn't," Toffit agreed, entirely unbothered. "Knew toffee, though.
Knew it better than I've ever known anything else. That's not nothing,
even when you're guessing." He turned in a slow circle, taking in his
small drifting kingdom — a dozen toffee-dipped trees, a listing vat, and
rather a lot of sea in every direction. "Suppose I'll need a sail."

"Or a rudder," Alex called back, already looking at the angle of the
current pulling gently at the little island's edge, already thinking
about it the way he thought about everything — carefully, and for as
long as it took.

"Or BOTH," said Felix, who had waded in up to his knees without anyone
noticing and was now holding up a broken branch like it might personally
solve the problem.

Toffit laughed, a big, warm sound that carried easily across the water.
"Both, then. Tell you what — you two ever fancy toffee apples again,
you won't need the harbour stall. Just look for the floating orchard.
I'll be about somewhere. Never quite sure where, these days — that's
rather the fun of it."

The island — Amberdrift, the pickers were already calling it, the name
passing from mouth to mouth along the shoreline before anyone had quite
decided to name it at all — drifted a little further out as they
watched, Toffit already elbow-deep in rigging something between two
trees that looked suspiciously like the beginnings of a sail.

The old picker who hadn't wanted to leave his basket was still watching
the water, apples forgotten. "Well," he said slowly. "That's not how I
thought that would go."

"Us neither," said Felix.

"But it's all right, though," Alex said — not a question, working it
out loud the way he worked most things out. "The orchard's still here.
Everyone's still here. Toffit's just... somewhere else now. Somewhere
new."

Felix looked out at the little drifting island, its sail half-rigged,
its owner whistling as he worked, growing smaller and stranger and more
wonderful by the minute.

"I liked him better as an island than a person," he decided.

"He's both," said Alex. "That's rather the whole thing, isn't it. You
can lose the bit you were trying to save, and it still turns out all
right — just not the way you thought it would."

They stayed at the shoreline until Amberdrift was only a bright, steaming
speck against the horizon. Then they went back up the row to finish the
harvest, baskets refilled, the rest of the orchard exactly as it had
always been — dipped, dressed, and waiting to be picked before the
afternoon sun came for it.

P.S. Felix ate eleven toffee apples over the course of the harvest and
was sick almost immediately afterward, an outcome Alex had predicted
with a riddle at apple number four that nobody had bothered to solve in
time.

P.P.S. That night, when Mummy asked how the harvest had gone, Alex
thought about it for a long moment before answering. "We did everything
right," he said finally, "and it still didn't work. And it was okay
anyway." Felix, half asleep already, mumbled his agreement into his
pillow — something that might have been "toffee" or might have been
"okay" — and by then it hardly mattered which.
