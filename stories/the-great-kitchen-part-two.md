The jigsaw piece arrived three days later, and everyone knew at once that it wasn't an ordinary one.

It was warm before anyone even touched it — warmer than any piece had ever been — and instead of showing a place, it showed nothing at all. Just a small, curling symbol, the exact same one from the corner of Nib's map, painted in ink so dark it looked almost black.

"That's it," said Alex, quiet and certain. "That's the mark."

Nobody argued about putting it in. Elfine simply reached over and pressed it into the gap herself, and the puzzle didn't just glow this time — it seemed to breathe, gold light pulsing slowly in and out like something waking up after a very long sleep.

• • •

They landed, not on an island this time, but on the deck of Nib's own little boat, bobbing gently on calm water. Nib was already there waiting for them, and she looked different from how they remembered her — no papers trailing behind her, no pencils tucked haphazardly behind her ears. Just Nib, sitting very still, with two things laid carefully across her knees: her own half-finished map, and her mother's old, careworn one.

"I couldn't stop thinking about it," she said, before anyone could ask. "After you left. I got out everything of hers I still have." She smoothed a hand over the older map. "There's more than just the mark, if you know where to look. She wrote things in the margins. Little notes to herself. I never really read them properly before — I think I didn't want to. But I did, last night. All of them."

"What did they say?" asked Marion, gently.

Nib turned the old map around so they could see. Scrawled in faded, careful handwriting along one edge, half-hidden beneath a smudge of old ink, were four words: The Great Kitchen. Someday.

"She called it that," Nib said. "The place everything comes from. Every island in all the Isles of Yum — she believed they all started there, somehow, a very long time ago, and that it's still out there, still making new ones, even now. She spent years trying to find it."

"But she never did," said Felix.

"I don't actually know that anymore," said Nib. "I always assumed she'd failed. That's what I've thought this whole time — that she ran out of time, or ran out of luck, and I was left to finish something impossible." She hesitated. "But look at this bit."

She pointed to another line, smaller, tucked into the very corner beside the symbol itself:

Some things aren't unfinished. Some things are just waiting for the right person to finish them with you.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"She wasn't just my mother," Nib said finally, very quietly. "She was training me. The whole time. I think — I think she wanted this part to be mine to find, not hers." She smoothed the old map flat again. "Her name was Cardamom, by the way. I don't think I ever told you that."

• • •

"Do you know how to get there?" Elfine asked.

Nib nodded, though she looked more nervous than certain. "There's a route marked, right at the very edge of the map, past every island anyone's ever charted. It's a long way. Further than I've ever sailed alone." She looked up at the four of them. "I don't want to go alone this time. Will you come?"

"Obviously," said Felix, as though the question barely needed asking.

Nib almost smiled — a small, real one, the first they'd properly seen from her — and began to roll up both maps together, old and new, side by side.

"Then let's go and finish it," she said. "Together, like she wrote."

• • •

The route, once Nib had it properly unrolled, turned out to be considerably longer than any of them had pictured — past Fish Finger Reef, past the Candy Floss Clouds, past even the very edge of the map they already knew, into waters marked only in Cardamom's careful, faded handwriting.

"Who's steering?" asked Felix, eyeing the little boat's single oar with some concern.

"The boat knows the way better than I do," said Nib, which was not, strictly speaking, an answer, but turned out to be entirely true. The moment she set the oar in the water and let go, the boat pulled itself steadily forward, cutting a smooth path through water that grew stranger and stranger the further they went — first pale gold, like weak tea, then a deep, glossy brown, like the inside of a very good biscuit tin.

"It smells like everything at once," said Marion, breathing in. "Chocolate, and toast, and — is that gravy?"

"That's rather the point, I think," said Nib. "We're getting close to where it all starts."

They passed a small, floating patch of sea that fizzed gently, like the top of a fizzy drink left open too long, and Felix leaned so far over the side to investigate that Nib had to grab the back of his jumper without even looking up from the map.

"Don't touch it," she said. "Last time someone touched an Unmapped Fizz, they came up smelling of lemonade for a fortnight."

"That sounds brilliant," said Felix, entirely undeterred.

"It was, apparently, extremely itchy," said Nib.

Felix sat back down.

• • •

Further on, they passed a cluster of small islands that didn't appear on any map at all — a lopsided one made entirely of burnt toast, looking rather sorry for itself; another no bigger than a dinner plate, made of a single perfect strawberry; a third that seemed to be nothing but steam, shaped roughly like a teapot, and gone again within moments.

"What are those?" Alex asked, watching the toast island drift past.

"Mistakes," said Nib. "Or not-quite-rights. Things that got cooked up but didn't turn out properly, so they never became real islands — they just sort of drift, out here at the edges, until they fade away completely."

"That's a bit sad," said Marion, watching the small burnt island bob past.

"Mother didn't think so," said Nib. "She used to say even a mistake had to come from somewhere trying its best. That's rather comforting, actually, when you think about it properly."

Elfine, who had been quietly tracking their route against the old map this whole time, looked up.

"We're nearly there," she said. "According to this, anyway. The route ends just past those clouds."

• • •

Ahead of them, rising suddenly out of a bank of low, warm mist, stood the outline of something enormous — taller than any island they'd yet seen, shaped, unmistakably, like an enormous, ancient, iron oven door.

Nib went very quiet.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's really it."

• • •

Up close, the oven door was even more enormous than it had seemed from a distance — taller than a house, its iron surface warm to the touch, patterned all over with the same curling symbol from the map, repeated again and again in careful, ancient etching.

There was no handle. No hinge that anyone could see. Just a single line of words, worked into the metal in letters that glowed faintly gold when Nib's boat drew close enough:

Not everyone who is ready may enter. Only those who understand what makes something worth finishing.

Beneath the words sat a small round dial, like the timer on an oven, with no numbers on it at all — just a single arrow, currently pointing at nothing.

"It's a question," said Elfine, studying it. "Not a lock. A question we have to answer properly, or it won't turn."

"What's the question, though?" said Felix, running his hand along the warm metal. "It just says the one thing."

Nib looked close to tears, though not sad ones — the nervous, hopeful kind. "Mother's notes stop right here," she said. "This is as far as she ever got. She wrote 'the door asks something, and I don't yet know the answer' — and that's it. That's the very last thing she ever wrote about this place."

• • •

Everyone looked at the dial. Nobody moved.

It was Alex who finally spoke, slowly, working it out the way Alex always did — quietly, and a little apart from the others, watching the whole shape of the problem rather than any one piece of it.

"It's not really asking what makes something worth finishing," Alex said. "I think that's the wrong way round. I think it's asking what makes something worth starting, even if you might not be the one who finishes it."

"What do you mean?" asked Marion.

"Think about it," said Alex. "Cardamom didn't finish the map. She didn't finish this door, even. But she still started it. She still tried, even knowing she might not be the one to get all the way through. That's not failing. That's — that's the opposite of failing, actually. That's the bravest bit."

Nib had gone very still.

"So the answer isn't a word," Alex went on. "It's not something we say out loud at all. I think it's just — doing it. Starting something you might not finish, because it's worth starting anyway." Alex paused, thinking of something simpler to say it with. "Like when Felix pushed the meatball. He didn't know for certain it would come loose. He just knew it was worth pushing anyway, because not trying definitely wouldn't work. I think the door just wants to see if that's true about us too."

Nobody was entirely sure what to do with that, until Elfine, watching the dial rather than the door, said quietly: "Then someone has to turn it. Not know the answer. Just turn it, and trust that the reason we came is enough."

"I'll do it," said Nib, before anyone else could offer, her voice shaking only a little. She stepped forward, placed one hand on the small dial, and turned.

• • •

For a moment, nothing happened at all, and Nib's face fell, just slightly, bracing for the door to stay shut forever.

Then the arrow moved on its own, swinging slowly round and round the dial's blank face, faster and faster, until it settled, at last, pointing straight up — and the enormous iron door groaned, shuddered, and began, impossibly, to swing inward, releasing a great warm gust of air that smelled of everything all at once: chocolate and gravy and toast and strawberries and something else besides, something none of them had words for yet, something that smelled, more than anything, like the very beginning of things.

"It opened," Nib breathed, staring through the widening gap at the golden light pouring out from within. "It actually opened."

• • •

Inside was nothing like any of them had pictured, and somehow exactly what it should have been.

It was vast — impossibly vast, far bigger than the little oven-door had suggested from outside — a great warm cavern lit by a soft golden glow that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere at once. Enormous mixing bowls the size of ponds sat scattered across the floor, some empty, some gently stirring themselves with no one visibly stirring them at all. Rivers of half-formed things wound between them — a stream of something that might become chocolate, if it kept going a little longer; a slow-drifting cloud that hadn't quite decided if it wanted to be candy floss or simply weather.

"It's not finished," said Marion, looking around in wonder. "None of it. It's all still becoming things."

"That's rather the whole point," said Nib softly, holding her mother's map against her chest like something precious. "It's not a place that gets finished. It's a place that keeps starting."

• • •

They walked slowly through the cavern, past a small bubbling pool that smelled unmistakably of custard, past a half-formed hill that hadn't yet decided if it would end up as a Yorkshire pudding or something nobody had tasted yet. In the very centre, on a raised platform surrounded by the largest mixing bowl of all, stood a single, small, ordinary-looking wooden spoon, resting exactly where someone had left it.

Nib stopped walking entirely.

"That's hers," she said, very quietly. "That's my mother's spoon. She always carried it. I thought — I thought it was lost, when she—" She didn't finish the sentence, and nobody made her.

• • •

Felix, who had been unusually quiet through most of this, finally spoke.

"Maybe she wasn't lost," he said. "Maybe she just got here. And maybe she knew this was as far as she needed to go, because she'd already given you everything else you needed to finish it."

Nib looked at him for a long moment, then walked slowly up to the platform and picked up the spoon. It was warm — properly warm, the way the jigsaw pieces always were — and the moment her fingers closed around it, the great mixing bowl beside the platform gave a slow, contented stir, all on its own, as though something enormous and ancient had simply been waiting for the right hand to hold it again.

"She left it for me," Nib said, and this time she did cry, though she was smiling at the same time, which seemed to be entirely allowed here.

"What happens now?" asked Elfine gently.

Nib looked around at the vast, golden, endlessly-becoming cavern — at the half-formed rivers and the self-stirring bowls and the whole enormous, unfinished, beautiful place her mother had spent her whole life trying to reach.

"Now," she said, turning the spoon over in her hands, "I think I finish what she started. Not all at once. Just — properly, this time. One island at a time, for as long as it takes."

• • •

They didn't stay long after that — the Great Kitchen, Nib explained, wasn't the sort of place you lingered in, not properly, not yet. "It'll still be here," she said, tucking the spoon carefully into her satchel alongside both maps. "That's rather the point of it. It's not going anywhere."

The great iron door swung shut behind them with a soft, warm groan, the glowing symbol fading gently back to ordinary metal, and the little boat carried them back out through the strange golden-brown water, past the mistake-islands still drifting at the edges of the map, past the fizzing patch Felix was still not allowed to touch, back towards the world of proper islands and proper coastlines they knew.

Nobody said very much on the way back. It wasn't an uncomfortable quiet — more the kind that comes after something enormous has happened and everyone's still turning it over, gently, in their own way.

"Will you keep exploring?" Marion asked eventually. "Now that you've been?"

"I think I have to," said Nib. "But not to prove anything anymore. Just because I want to see what's there." She smiled, properly, the same real smile from before. "That's rather different, isn't it? Doing something because you're scared not to, and doing it because you actually want to."

"Very different," Elfine agreed.

• • •

Back at the sandwich island, Nib unrolled both maps side by side one final time — her mother's old, careworn one, and her own, fresher and still growing. Carefully, with her mother's own spoon dipped in a little pot of glowing ink, she drew one last careful line, connecting the corner where the symbol had been to the rest of the map entirely, filling it in at last — not blank, not just a symbol anymore, but a proper place, labelled in her own careful, steady handwriting: The Great Kitchen. Found together, at last.

"There," she said, sitting back to look at it. "Finished. Well — that bit, anyway. There's rather a lot more Isles out there I haven't got round to yet."

"Good," said Felix. "That means there's more adventures."

Nib laughed — a proper, easy laugh, nothing like the frantic scrambling energy from when they'd first met her — and rolled the map up carefully to keep it safe.

"I rather think there will be," she said.

• • •

The jigsaw piece pulled them gently home, the warm golden light of the Great Kitchen fading behind them, replaced by the ordinary warmth of their own kitchen, where the rain had finally stopped and the sun was doing its best to come out.

P.S. — Nib sent, some weeks later, a small parcel through the jigsaw piece itself, containing four tiny wooden spoons, one for each of them, each one slightly too warm to the touch, as though it had spent time somewhere rather magical before it arrived.

P.P.S. — "Some things you don't finish because you can't," Mummy said, when they told her the whole story that evening. "You finish them because someone loved you enough to leave you the rest of the way to go." Alex thought about that for a long time, and decided, quietly, that this was probably the best story any of them had brought home yet.
