The sea had been perfectly ordinary blue-green for ages, and then, without anyone quite noticing the exact moment it happened, it wasn't.

"Alex," said Felix, resting his oar across his knees, "the water's gone weird."

It had. It was thickening around the little rowing boat, turning paler and warmer-looking with every stroke, blue-green fading up into a heavy, glowing ivory-gold, like the whole sea was slowly stirring itself into a very large cup of tea. The waves had changed too — instead of chopping and slapping against the hull, they rolled, slow and round-shouldered, the way something thick moves instead of something thin. Rowing through it took real effort now, the oars dragging heavier with every pull, like stirring a spoon through something only just pourable.

"It's not weird," said Alex, who had been watching it happen for the last several minutes with great interest, and was rowing more slowly than Felix to make it easier going. "It's changing on purpose. Watch — there."

A round, smooth shape the size of a beach ball bobbed past on their left, pale and glossy. A moment later, on their right, a knobblier one drifted by, cratered all over like a little moon.

"Nut-islets," said Alex. "I think we're between two different kinds of something."

Felix leaned out to bump the smooth one with his oar, just because it was there, and it rocked lazily away, entirely unbothered.

Ahead, low ridges broke the surface here and there — pale, crusty-looking things, like the tops of biscuits poking through. "Reef," said Alex, and they steered wide around it without needing to discuss it further, the boat's flat bottom skidding a little on the thick water as it turned.

The closer they got to shore, the warmer everything felt — not just the water now, but the air itself, a soft haze rising off the sea like steam off a bath. And right at the very edge, where the shallows met the sand, something caught the light and shimmered — a thin, rainbowy sheen skating across the surface, there and gone, there and gone.

"Ooh," said Felix, delighted, and promptly forgot about it entirely, because that was the moment they scraped up onto the beach.

• • •

The island split cleanly in two, and you could tell which was which without anyone explaining a thing.

To the left, smooth glossy hills rolled gently down toward the shore, honey-brown and gleaming, so polished-looking that Felix immediately wanted to lie down and slide. A little painted sign, driven into the sand at a cheerful angle, read: SATIN SLOPES — The Only Proper Way To Finish An Almond.

To the right, the ground turned craggy and uneven, boulders scattered everywhere like the whole hillside had been left out to go crunchy — knobbled, sharp-edged, perfect for scrambling over. Its sign read: RATTLENUT ROCKS — Smooth Is A Nice Try, But.

Between the two, at the very bottom where both hillsides met, stood a well unlike any well either of them had ever seen. Instead of a simple bucket on a rope, a long wooden arm rose up from it, taller than Felix, tipping slowly down toward the water and lifting slowly back up again, over and over, steady as breathing.

Nod. Pause. Nod. Pause.

"What's it doing?" asked Felix.

A Satin Slopes resident happened to be passing — smooth, glossy, faintly the shape of a large glossy drop — and answered without even slowing down. "Oh, that's just the well. It's always nodded like that. Nobody knows why, and nobody's ever needed to."

"Right," said Felix, who had already stopped listening, because several residents from both sides had begun arguing rather loudly a little further up the beach, and arguing was much more interesting than a nodding well.

• • •

"It has to be smooth," a Satin Slopes resident was saying, arms folded, glossy all over with the particular shine of someone very sure of themselves. "It's always been smooth. Grandmother's grandmother pressed it smooth, and hers before that. That's what Harvest Day is *for*."

"It has to be crunchy," said a Rattlenut resident, craggy-faced and just as certain, standing his ground on a boulder like it was a matter of principle. "Crunchy's the proper finish. Always has been. Last year you lot tried to sneak a smooth batch into the shared harvest basket, and we're not having that again."

"It was an *accident*," said the Satin Slopes resident, going a shade glossier with indignation.

"NEITHER of you will finish ANYTHING," said a third voice, wringing its hands, "if you don't harvest the grove together by sundown, and then it'll be nobody's almond butter, smooth or crunchy, because it'll all have gone off!"

Nobody seemed to be listening to that part.

Felix wandered straight into the middle of it, the way Felix always did, and within about ninety seconds had picked a side without meaning to — mostly because a Rattlenut resident pressed something small and round into his hand to demonstrate a point about texture, and Felix liked how it felt.

"What is this?" Felix asked, turning it over. It was smooth almost everywhere, but with one dented, crunchy little patch, like it hadn't quite decided what it wanted to be.

"Worry-almond," said the resident, distracted, already back to arguing. "Everyone here's got one. Helps you think."

Felix put it in his pocket and forgot about it entirely, which was, as it happened, exactly the wrong thing to forget.

• • •

Alex did not join the argument.

Alex stood a little apart from it, the way he usually did at first in a new place, watching rather than talking, working out the shape of things before deciding where he fit into them. And because he was standing quietly instead of shouting, he was the only one who actually watched the well properly.

Nod. Pause. Nod. Pause.

Everyone else had decided long ago that the well just moved like that. Alex wasn't so sure. He liked things that had an explanation waiting to be found — the kind of thing you could turn into a riddle once you understood it properly — and a motion with no reason behind it was exactly the sort of loose thread he couldn't leave alone.

He walked right up to the well and looked down. Far below, past where the wooden arm dipped into shadow, the nodding was slower but clearer, and underneath it, very faint, something that might have been humming.

There was a narrow gap beside the well's stone base — not quite big enough for a grown-up, exactly the right size for a five-year-old — where the arm's post disappeared down into the dark.

Alex went to fetch Felix. Felix did not come, on account of being extremely occupied lending his professional worry-almond opinion to Rattlenut Rocks. So Alex went alone, the way he usually preferred to work anyway when something needed real thinking, and climbed down into the dark by himself.

• • •

The tunnel opened, after a long, careful climb, into somewhere enormous.

It wasn't dark, not really — a soft golden light seemed to come from the walls themselves, or perhaps from the wide, slow spring bubbling up in the middle of the cavern floor, pale and warm and gently glowing, exactly the same colour as the sea outside. Wooden posts rose up from the cavern floor in several places, disappearing into the ceiling toward the surface, each one nodding gently up and down on its own slow rhythm.

And in the middle of it all, curled comfortably beside the spring with an enormous curved claw resting in the water, was something so large that for one whole second, Alex forgot to be anything other than astonished.

It looked up at him slowly, in no hurry at all, with the kindest, sleepiest eyes Alex had ever seen on anything. One enormous claw was braced against a long wooden post that rose up out of sight through the cavern's ceiling — and as Alex watched, Marzipan leaned their whole weight down on it, slow and steady, then eased back up again.

Nod. Pause. Nod. Pause.

"You're the well," said Alex, understanding all at once. "You're what makes it move."

"Oh," it said, in a voice like a yawn deciding to become a sentence. "Visitors. That hasn't happened in rather a while." Another slow push, another slow release. "I am the well, rather, yes. Or the well's the bit of me that pokes through. I'm Marzipan. I look after the—"

That was the exact moment Felix arrived — upside down, mostly, having finally noticed his worry-almond was missing, followed it to the gap by the well, and tumbled down after it rather faster than planned, landing in a heap at the bottom.

"FISH?" Felix cut in, scrambling upright, because guessing felt more useful than waiting.

Marzipan didn't stop, and didn't seem to mind. "...spring," they finished, exactly as unhurried as before. "I look after the spring."

"OW — that's a very big—" Felix looked up properly. "That is a VERY big—"

"Sloth," said Marzipan helpfully. "Yes. I get that a lot. Well — I don't, actually. Nobody's really been down to say."

Felix's alarm lasted approximately one second before turning entirely into delight, on account of Marzipan's face being possibly the friendliest thing he had ever seen, claws and all.

• • •

"You made the sea go golden," said Alex slowly, working it through. "And the shimmer, at the shore."

"That's just where the fresh spring meets the sea," said Marzipan. "It does that. Rather pretty, I always think, though I don't get to see it myself, not from down here."

"And the well," said Alex. "It's you. Working the arm, the whole time."

"Every day," said Marzipan. "Both sides, equal shares, same spring. Have done for longer than I can properly remember." One claw dipped gently into the water and, with great care, nudged a stone aside to let more flow through. It was slow, careful work, the kind you could only do properly if you weren't in any hurry at all.

"But nobody ever comes down here?" asked Felix, aghast, as if this were the single worst thing he had ever heard.

Marzipan considered this with the unhurried patience of someone who had clearly considered it many times before. "Most people just want what comes out of the tap," they said, not unkindly, not sadly either — just plainly, the way you'd say the sky was up. "Nobody's ever followed the sound to find out where it came from. That's alright, though. I like it down here. Quiet, and warm, and there's always something that needs tending."

"But you must get thanked," said Alex. "Surely. For all of it."

Marzipan didn't answer straight away. They shifted the claw against the post, leaned into another slow push, and when they finally spoke again, it came out even slower than before, like the words themselves didn't much want to be said.

"Nobody's really ever—"

Felix cut in again. "—SEEN you?"

Marzipan carried on as though the interruption had simply not happened. "...asked," they said, gently, several whole seconds later than Felix expected. "Nobody's ever asked how it feels, doing it on your own. Not unkindly. Just — nobody thought to."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Even Felix, who usually had something to say about everything, didn't.

"That's not the same as being sad about it, mind," Marzipan added, a little more briskly, as though worried they'd made too much of it. "I do like the quiet. I chose it, this, down here. I just hadn't noticed, until just now, saying it out loud, that liking your own company and never once being asked about it are two rather different things."

Alex thought about that for a long moment, the way he thought about most things — properly, without rushing it. Beside him, the nodding post paused a fraction longer than usual before lifting again, as if Marzipan were thinking it over too.

• • •

They climbed back up together, Marzipan's directions echoing patiently behind them, and emerged into an argument that had, if anything, gotten louder.

"SMOOTH—"

"CRUNCHY—"

"THE GROVE IS GOING TO OVERRIPEN WHILE YOU BOTH—"

Alex stood at the edge of the well and thought. He had a riddle forming already — he could feel it arriving the way they always did, piece by piece, the shape of an idea folding itself into a question instead of a plain answer, because that was simply how his head liked to work things out loud.

"Everyone," he said. It wasn't very loud, but something in how steadily he said it made both sides actually stop and look.

"I've got a riddle," said Alex. "It's about something that's in every single bit of almond butter either of you have ever made. Smooth or crunchy. Every batch, every year."

A Satin Slopes resident frowned. A Rattlenut resident folded his arms, suspicious but curious despite himself.

"Go on then," he said.

Alex took a breath.

"I'm in everything you've ever finished," he said, "but I've never finished myself. You use me every single day, and you've never once asked my name. What am I?"

Silence. Real silence — the good kind, the kind where everyone's actually thinking instead of arguing, which hadn't happened on this beach in quite some time.

A Satin Slopes resident's eyes went wide.

"The well," she said slowly.

A Rattlenut resident's eyes went wide at exactly the same moment.

"The spring," he said. "Underneath. Whoever's — whoever's *down there.*"

They looked at each other properly for what felt like the first time all day.

• • •

Down below, Marzipan heard the cheer that went up through the old wooden post, muffled and strange but unmistakably a cheer, and paused mid-nod, one claw resting on the wood.

Up above, both territories had gathered properly around the well for the first time in memory, Satin Slopes and Rattlenut Rocks residents standing shoulder to shoulder rather than glaring across a shared piece of ground.

"We should say thank you," said Alex, "properly. Not just once."

"We could leave something," suggested Felix, who had a very clear idea of what that something should be, on account of the worry-almond currently sitting in his pocket. He fished it out and set it carefully on the rim of the well. "For Marzipan. Every year."

It caught the light for a second, smooth on one side, crunchy on the other, exactly the right shape for what it was about to mean.

By the time the grove was harvested — together, at last, both hillsides working side by side while the light went gold and low — a little pile of thank-yous had gathered at the well's edge: a smooth almond from one side, a crunchy one from the other, set down by hands that had never quite met properly before today.

Down below, Marzipan found them at the end of the day, and, for the first time in longer than they could remember, smiled slowly enough that it took almost a full minute to reach both ends of their face.

• • •

Felix and Alex rowed back out over water that was already fading gently from gold back toward blue, the shimmer at the shoreline glinting once, brightly, like a wink.

"Do you think they'll remember?" Felix asked. "To keep saying thank you?"

"I think so," said Alex. "Once you've actually followed a sound all the way down to find out what it is, it's rather hard to go back to not noticing it."

P.S. — Felix's worry-almond, once safely delivered to the well, was almost immediately replaced by a new one, which he named Kevin, and which accompanied him on every adventure for exactly one week before being lost, with great dignity, down the back of the sofa at home.

P.P.S. — "It's a funny thing," Alex said that evening, half asleep, "how easy it is to only notice the bit you can see." Nobody was quite sure if he was still talking about the island, or about something else entirely, and Alex didn't say, and nobody asked him to.
